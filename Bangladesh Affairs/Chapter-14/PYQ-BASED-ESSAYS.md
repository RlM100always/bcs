## Essay 1 — Globalization: Opportunities and Challenges for Bangladesh

Globalization is the growing interdependence of countries through trade, investment, technology, information, labour mobility and ideas. For Bangladesh, it has opened access to export markets, overseas employment, remittance flows, foreign investment and international knowledge. Yet it also transmits external shocks, exposes workers and firms to global competition, and may deepen inequality where domestic capabilities are weak. The central policy question is therefore not whether Bangladesh should accept or reject globalization, but how it can participate more productively, fairly and sustainably.

The ready-made garment industry demonstrates the benefits of integration into global value chains. International demand has supported manufacturing, jobs and export earnings. Migrant workers connect households to foreign labour markets, while remittances contribute to family welfare and external-sector receipts. Foreign investment and digital services can add capital, technology, management practices and new markets. Multilateral institutions also provide frameworks for trade, development finance and international cooperation.

However, integration creates vulnerabilities. Heavy reliance on a narrow export base can expose firms to changes in buyer demand and trade rules. Global increases in fuel, food or input prices can raise domestic costs. Migrant workers may face recruitment debt, contract violations or unsafe work. Smaller firms can struggle to meet technical and environmental standards, while the benefits of growth may concentrate in better-connected regions and skilled groups. Global production can also impose environmental costs.

Bangladesh should respond through export and market diversification, higher domestic value addition, skills development, technology adoption and better logistics. Labour rights, safe migration, social protection and support for small firms are essential to distribute gains more widely. Green industrialization, energy efficiency and climate adaptation can improve resilience and competitiveness. Economic diplomacy should anticipate changes in trade preferences and support a smooth transition through international rules.

In conclusion, globalization creates opportunities but does not guarantee inclusive development. Bangladesh’s gains will depend on productive capacity, capable institutions, fair rules and evidence-based policy. The goal should be resilient, inclusive and sustainable participation in the global economy.

## Essay 2 — Export Diversification and Bangladesh’s Long-Term Competitiveness

Export diversification reduces vulnerability to dependence on a limited number of products or markets. It can raise domestic value addition, improve resilience to external shocks and create opportunities for skilled employment. For Bangladesh, diversification is not simply a list of potential industries; it requires firms to meet international standards, deliver reliably and compete on productivity, quality and innovation.

A concentrated export structure creates exposure to shifts in consumer demand, buyer sourcing decisions and changes in trade preferences. Firms may also face rising compliance costs as environmental and labour requirements evolve. Diversification can broaden the base of export earnings and encourage stronger domestic supplier networks. Potential opportunities exist in sectors such as pharmaceuticals, agro-processing, leather goods, light engineering and ICT services, but each requires evidence-based assessment of market demand, comparative advantage and capability.

Several policy measures are necessary. First, improve ports, customs, transport, energy reliability and digital trade procedures. Second, expand technical training and partnerships between industry and educational institutions. Third, strengthen testing laboratories, certification, research, design and product development. Fourth, provide small and medium-sized enterprises with access to finance, market information and technology. Fifth, use economic diplomacy to address market access, standards recognition and trade facilitation.

Diversification should be measured through indicators such as the share of new products and markets, domestic value addition, productivity, quality employment and survival of new exporters. Official Export Promotion Bureau data should be cited with the relevant fiscal year and unit. Announcing a new sector without evidence of competitiveness does not constitute a successful diversification strategy.

In conclusion, sustainable competitiveness requires a move from cost-based competition towards skills, reliability, innovation and value creation. A coordinated approach can reduce concentration risk while supporting higher-quality jobs and stronger domestic enterprises.

## Essay 3 — Globalization, Labour Rights and Inclusive Development

Global economic integration can expand employment, connect workers to international markets and support the transfer of skills. However, workers may not receive a fair share of the gains if bargaining power is unequal, employment is insecure or social protection is weak. Inclusive globalization therefore requires labour rights to be treated as a foundation of sustainable competitiveness rather than an obstacle to trade.

Bangladesh’s export industries illustrate both sides of integration. International demand can create jobs and stimulate investment, while global buyers may require better quality, safety and environmental practices. Yet price pressure, informal work, weak grievance mechanisms and unequal access to training can leave workers vulnerable. Migrant workers can earn more abroad but may incur high recruitment costs or experience contract substitution, wage theft and unsafe conditions.

Policy should strengthen occupational safety, timely wage payment, lawful worker representation, grievance handling and effective inspection. Recruitment agencies should be monitored, migration costs made transparent and workers given access to verified contracts and consular assistance. Skills certification and recognition can improve mobility and earnings. Social protection and retraining should help workers affected by automation or shifts in global demand.

Businesses also have responsibilities. Supply-chain due diligence, transparent purchasing practices and realistic delivery schedules can reduce incentives for abusive labour practices. Public policy should encourage compliance while ensuring that smaller suppliers can access finance and technical support. Labour reforms should be assessed through evidence on workplace safety, wages, grievance resolution and worker experience.

In conclusion, inclusive globalization must connect market access with decent work, fair opportunity and effective remedies. Economic success is more durable when workers share in productivity gains and can exercise their rights.

## Essay 4 — Multilateral Institutions and Bangladesh’s Development

Multilateral institutions influence global trade, financial stability and development cooperation. Their mandates differ: the World Trade Organization provides a framework for trade rules and negotiations; the International Monetary Fund focuses on monetary cooperation and macroeconomic stability; and the World Bank, Asian Development Bank and Islamic Development Bank support development through finance, knowledge and institutional assistance. Understanding these differences helps Bangladesh engage with each institution according to national priorities.

Such institutions can help finance infrastructure, strengthen capacity, support reforms and provide comparative evidence. Trade rules can improve predictability, while technical assistance can help agencies improve data, standards and project implementation. Development finance can be particularly important where domestic resources are insufficient for long-term investment.

Nevertheless, external support does not automatically produce successful outcomes. Poor project preparation, weak procurement, implementation delays, debt risks, limited local ownership or inadequate safeguards can reduce benefits. The value of a project should be assessed by its economic and social returns, transparency, environmental effects and contribution to domestic capability—not simply by the amount of finance secured.

Bangladesh should improve project appraisal, public disclosure, procurement integrity and independent monitoring. Debt sustainability and contingent liabilities should be evaluated transparently. Institutions should coordinate with domestic agencies and stakeholders, while national authorities retain clear responsibility for policy choices and results. Public data should enable citizens to assess commitments and outcomes.

In conclusion, multilateral cooperation is most useful when aligned with national development goals and supported by capable domestic institutions. External finance and expertise should build lasting local capacity, not replace it.

## Essay 5 — LDC Graduation, Global Trade and Economic Resilience

Graduation from the least developed country category is an important development milestone, but it also requires preparation for changing international treatment and competition. The timing and terms of specific trade preferences depend on the relevant international rules and partner-country arrangements, so claims about tariffs or transition periods must be checked against current official sources.

Bangladesh should prepare by raising productivity, diversifying products and markets, improving standards compliance and increasing domestic value addition. Firms need clear information about rules of origin, certification, customs procedures and evolving buyer requirements. Smaller exporters may need technical assistance and affordable finance to adapt. Workers require skills training as production technologies and market requirements change.

Economic resilience also depends on reducing vulnerability to global shocks. Reliable energy, efficient ports, strong financial institutions, climate adaptation and targeted social protection can help businesses and households absorb disruptions. Economic diplomacy should seek predictable market access, cooperation on standards and support for technology and skills. Policy choices should be based on current trade data and scenario analysis rather than assumptions that existing preferences will continue unchanged.

Progress should be measured through export diversification, productivity, quality employment, domestic value addition, SME participation and resilience to external shocks. Government agencies should publish timely data and coordinate trade, industry, education and labour policies.

In conclusion, LDC graduation should be approached as a managed transition rather than a one-time event. Early preparation, credible institutions and productive investment can help Bangladesh convert the milestone into more sustainable global competitiveness.
