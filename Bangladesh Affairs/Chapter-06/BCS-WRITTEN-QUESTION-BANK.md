## Q1. Define natural resources and classify them with examples from Bangladesh. (5 marks)

**Model answer**

Natural resources are materials, organisms and ecological systems occurring in nature that support human life, production and social well-being. They include not only marketable commodities but also services such as water purification, soil formation, pollination, flood regulation and carbon storage.

They may be classified as:

1. **Renewable resources:** resources that can regenerate if their rate of use remains within ecological limits—for example, fish stocks, forests, freshwater and solar energy. Renewable does not mean inexhaustible: overfishing or forest degradation can reduce them.
2. **Non-renewable resources:** geological resources that form over very long periods, such as natural gas, coal, limestone, hard rock and other minerals.
3. **Biotic resources:** derived from living organisms, including forests, fisheries, livestock and biodiversity.
4. **Abiotic resources:** land, water, sunlight, air and mineral deposits.
5. **Ecosystem services:** benefits provided by functioning ecosystems, including coastal protection, water regulation, soil fertility and habitat for wildlife.

In Bangladesh, agricultural land, river systems, wetlands, forests, marine fisheries and natural gas have economic and social importance. Their value depends on access, quality, location and governance—not merely on physical quantity.

**Conclusion:** Resource policy must combine productive use with ecological limits, public access and intergenerational equity.

**Sources:** BPSC Written Syllabus, Unit 6 [S1]; UN SDGs [S2].

## Q2. What is sustainable resource management? Explain its core principles. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Sustainable resource management means using natural resources to meet present social and economic needs while maintaining ecosystem functions and the ability of future generations to meet their needs. It is not simply “using less”; it is about using resources within ecological limits and distributing benefits fairly.

Its core principles are:

- **Intergenerational equity:** avoid consuming or degrading resources in ways that shift excessive costs to future generations.
- **Ecological integrity:** maintain biodiversity, habitat connectivity, soil health and water quality.
- **Efficiency:** reduce losses, pollution and waste throughout production and consumption.
- **Precaution:** where serious or irreversible harm is plausible, uncertainty should not be used as a reason to delay reasonable safeguards.
- **Polluter pays:** those responsible for pollution should bear appropriate prevention, control and remediation costs.
- **Participation and transparency:** affected communities should have access to information and a meaningful role in decisions.
- **Equity and livelihoods:** conservation rules should recognise the rights and needs of farmers, fishers, forest-dependent people and other vulnerable groups.
- **Monitoring and adaptation:** compare outcomes with a baseline and revise policy when evidence changes.

For Bangladesh, these principles apply to river basins, agricultural land, the Sundarbans, fisheries, gas extraction and renewable energy. Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 provides a long-term framework for integrated water, land and climate-resilience planning, but its success depends on coordination, finance, maintenance and accountability.

**Conclusion:** Sustainable management is a continuing cycle of evidence, rules, responsible use, restoration and review.

**Sources:** BPSC [S1]; *Our Common Future* [S3]; Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 [S4].

## Q3. Discuss the importance of land and soil resources in Bangladesh and suggest measures to prevent degradation. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Land and soil underpin food production, settlements, industry, infrastructure, forests, wetlands and rural livelihoods. Fertile alluvial soils support major agricultural systems, while different landscapes—such as the Barind tract, Madhupur tract, hills, haors and coastal plains—require different management approaches.

Major pressures include conversion of agricultural land to housing and industry, unplanned urban expansion, erosion, salinity, waterlogging, excessive or poorly managed agrochemical use, loss of organic matter and unsustainable extraction of sand or other materials. Soil problems vary by region; a single remedy cannot work everywhere.

A sustainable policy should:

1. protect high-quality agricultural land through land-use planning and enforcement;
2. map soil type, salinity, erosion risk and land capability before approving major projects;
3. improve soil organic matter through suitable crop rotations, compost and residue management;
4. promote integrated pest management and responsible fertiliser use;
5. improve drainage and water management where salinity or waterlogging is a constraint;
6. restore eroded or degraded land with locally appropriate vegetation and engineering;
7. assess the cumulative effects of roads, embankments, urban development and extraction projects;
8. provide farmers with extension support, soil testing and incentives for conservation practices.

**Conclusion:** Land protection is not opposed to development. Good spatial planning and soil stewardship help maintain food security, livelihoods and resilient economic growth.

**Sources:** BPSC [S1]; Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 atlas [S4]; BBS Statistical Yearbook [S5].

## Q4. Analyse the causes and consequences of agricultural land conversion to non-agricultural uses in Bangladesh. Suggest remedies. (10/20 marks)

**Model answer**

Agricultural land conversion occurs when farmland is used for housing, roads, factories, commercial buildings, brickfields or other non-agricultural purposes. The issue was directly relevant to the 45th BCS Bangladesh Affairs paper, whose archive reports a question on the nature of agricultural land conversion and possible remedies [S6].

**Causes:** population growth and urbanisation increase demand for land; industrial and transport projects may be concentrated around productive agricultural zones; land-use enforcement can be weak; fragmented ownership and short-term price incentives can encourage conversion; and some local development plans fail to assess cumulative food-system effects.

**Consequences:** the loss of fertile land can reduce local production capacity, increase pressure on remaining farms, fragment agricultural landscapes and weaken water drainage. Poorly planned development may increase flood risk, heat, traffic and pollution. Conversion can also affect rural employment and the viability of agricultural supply chains.

**Remedies:** establish transparent land-use maps; protect high-quality agricultural soils; require environmental and social assessment for major projects; direct industrial expansion towards appropriately planned sites; use compact urban development and brownfield redevelopment; improve local-government planning and enforcement; publish conversion data; and consult affected communities. Land protection should be balanced with housing, industry and infrastructure needs rather than applied as an absolute ban everywhere.

**Conclusion:** A predictable land-use policy can reconcile development with food security. The key is to assess location, soil quality, ecological functions and alternatives before converting agricultural land.

**Source:** 45th BCS Written archive [S6]; BPSC [S1].

## Q5. Discuss the major challenges of water-resource management in Bangladesh. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Water supports drinking, sanitation, agriculture, fisheries, industry, navigation and ecosystems. Yet a river-rich country can still experience water stress because availability varies by season and region, and water quality can be poor.

Major challenges include seasonal floods and dry-season scarcity; riverbank erosion and sedimentation; industrial and municipal pollution; groundwater depletion or contamination in some locations; drainage congestion and waterlogging; salinity in coastal areas; loss of wetland connectivity; and the uncertainty associated with upstream flows and climate change. Water needs also compete across agriculture, cities, industry, navigation and ecosystems.

A sound response requires **Integrated Water Resources Management (IWRM)**: manage surface water and groundwater together; coordinate across the river basin; maintain environmental flows; protect wetlands and floodplains; improve wastewater treatment; monitor water quality; encourage efficient irrigation; recharge or conserve water where feasible; and share information across agencies and borders. Local users should participate in planning, especially where water rules affect farmers, fishers and low-income communities.

Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 can support long-term coordination, but implementation requires clear responsibilities, funding, maintenance and measurable results.

**Conclusion:** Water management must focus on quantity, quality, timing and equitable access—not simply the number of rivers or the volume of annual rainfall.

**Sources:** BPSC [S1]; BWDB [S7]; Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 [S4]; 47th BCS Written archive [S8].

## Q6. Why is transboundary water governance important for Bangladesh? Discuss the Teesta issue. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Bangladesh shares river systems with neighbouring countries. Water flow, sediment, flood peaks and dry-season availability cross political borders, so unilateral planning cannot fully manage a transboundary river. Cooperation is important for agriculture, drinking water, navigation, ecological flows, disaster preparedness and regional trust.

The Teesta is especially important in northern Bangladesh, where seasonal flow affects irrigation and livelihoods. Dry-season scarcity can intensify competition among irrigation, domestic use and ecosystem needs. The 45th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive reports a question on why a Teesta water-sharing agreement is needed and the actions taken by Bangladesh and India [S6].

A sustainable approach should include transparent hydrological data; joint monitoring; agreed procedures for drought and flood periods; technical discussion on seasonal demand and ecological flows; early-warning systems; dispute-resolution channels; and local consultation. Negotiation should be evidence-led and recognise both upstream and downstream needs, while protecting the interests of communities that depend on the river.

An agreement is most useful when it contains measurable provisions, monitoring arrangements, review mechanisms and procedures for exceptional hydrological conditions. Diplomatic announcements alone do not ensure implementation.

**Conclusion:** Transboundary water governance should be treated as long-term basin management rather than only a diplomatic event. Predictable cooperation can improve livelihoods, reduce uncertainty and support ecological resilience.

**Sources:** 45th BCS Written archive [S6]; BPSC [S1]; Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 [S4].

## Q7. Assess the role of Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 in sustainable resource management. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 is a long-term national planning framework that connects water management, land use, environmental sustainability, climate risks and economic development. Its value lies in treating Bangladesh as a dynamic delta where floods, droughts, river erosion, coastal hazards, sediment movement, urbanisation and ecosystem change interact.

The plan encourages integrated planning rather than isolated projects. For example, an embankment may protect one area but can alter drainage or sediment patterns elsewhere; a water project should therefore be assessed at basin and landscape scales. The plan’s atlas also brings together spatial information on flood risk, drought, land use, erosion and accretion, forests and other risk indicators [S4].

Its potential contributions include improved investment prioritisation, stronger inter-agency coordination, climate adaptation, water security, better spatial planning and a longer time horizon for infrastructure decisions.

However, a plan is not itself an outcome. Challenges include financing, coordination among ministries, local implementation capacity, data quality, maintenance of infrastructure, social safeguards and ensuring that benefits reach exposed communities. Projects should be monitored against outcomes, not only expenditure or construction milestones.

**Conclusion:** BDP 2100 is a useful framework, but its success depends on evidence-led decisions, public accountability, community participation and the ability to adapt as conditions change.

**Sources:** General Economics Division, Bangladesh Planning Commission [S4]; BWDB [S7].

## Q8. Explain the importance of forest resources in Bangladesh and identify major threats. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Forest resources provide timber and non-timber products, habitats for wildlife, carbon storage, soil protection, water regulation, recreation and livelihood opportunities. They also support ecological functions that are difficult to replace after degradation.

Bangladesh contains several forest types, including mangrove forests in the Sundarbans, hill forests in the southeast and northeast, and sal-dominated forest areas. Each type has different ecological characteristics and management needs. The Sundarbans are especially important for mangrove biodiversity, fisheries, local livelihoods and coastal ecological resilience.

Major threats include habitat loss and fragmentation, encroachment, illegal extraction, fires in some landscapes, pollution, unsustainable harvesting, infrastructure pressures and climate-related salinity or storm risks. Forest conservation can also fail if rules exclude forest-dependent people without providing viable alternatives.

Key responses include protecting and restoring native habitat; strengthening enforcement against illegal extraction; monitoring forest condition and biodiversity; supporting social forestry and community co-management; improving fire and disaster preparedness; recognising local and Indigenous knowledge and rights; and ensuring that restoration uses ecologically appropriate species rather than only counting planted trees.

**Conclusion:** Forest policy should measure ecological condition, biodiversity, restoration survival and livelihood outcomes—not only tree-planting totals or the nominal area under protection.

**Sources:** Bangladesh Forest Department [S9]; FAO sustainable forest management [S10]; UNESCO Sundarbans [S11].

## Q9. Why is the Sundarbans important, and how should it be conserved? (10 marks)

**Model answer**

The Sundarbans is a large mangrove ecosystem shared by Bangladesh and India and is recognised by UNESCO as a World Heritage property. It provides habitat for distinctive flora and fauna, supports fisheries and forest-dependent livelihoods, stores carbon and contributes to coastal ecological processes. Mangroves can reduce some wave and erosion impacts, although they cannot eliminate all cyclone or storm-surge risks.

Threats include changes in freshwater and salinity conditions, pollution, unsustainable resource use, habitat disturbance, climate change, shipping or industrial pressures in sensitive areas and conflict between conservation rules and local livelihoods.

A conservation strategy should maintain habitat connectivity and ecological flows; monitor salinity, water quality and key species; regulate fishing and forest extraction based on evidence; prevent and respond to oil or chemical spills; strengthen community-based co-management; provide alternative income and social protection where restrictions affect livelihoods; and use tourism limits and codes of conduct where necessary. Transboundary cooperation is important because ecological processes do not stop at the international border.

Protected-area rules should be transparent and enforced consistently. Conservation should also recognise local knowledge and ensure that communities receive a fair share of sustainable benefits.

**Conclusion:** The Sundarbans should be managed as a living ecosystem and livelihood landscape, not merely as a timber source or tourism destination.

**Sources:** UNESCO World Heritage Centre [S11]; Bangladesh Forest Department [S9]; UNCLOS marine protection provisions [S12].

## Q10. What are protected areas and Ecologically Critical Areas (ECAs)? Explain their importance. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

A **protected area** is a geographically defined area managed for conservation, such as a national park, wildlife sanctuary or marine protected area. An **Ecologically Critical Area (ECA)** is an area identified under Bangladesh’s environmental framework because ecological conditions or environmental pressures require special protection and management. The two concepts overlap in purpose but are not identical in legal designation or management arrangement.

The 47th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive reports a question asking candidates to define ECAs, explain their significance and describe Bangladesh’s ECAs [S8]. The examiner is therefore likely to expect definition, purpose, examples and implementation challenges—not merely a list of names.

Examples listed in a Department of Environment publication include the Sundarbans' 10-km landward periphery, Cox's Bazar–Teknaf Peninsula, St Martin's Island, Sonadia Island, Hakaluki Haor, Tanguar Haor, Marjat Baor, Gulshan–Baridhara Lake, Buriganga, Turag, Balu and Shitalakhya rivers, and the Jaflong–Dawki river area [S27]. This is a dated list, not a guarantee that no later notifications have been issued; check the latest official gazette and DoE page before presenting a count as current.

ECAs and protected areas can safeguard habitats, breeding grounds, water quality, biodiversity and ecosystem services. They can also support research, education, nature-based tourism and disaster resilience. Their designation is only the first step: management plans, enforcement, monitoring, public information and local participation are necessary.

The Forest Department’s official page, updated in May 2026, reported 58 terrestrial and marine protected areas in its listed system and gave the area totals used on that page [S9]. This is a separate protected-area system, not the ECA count. Counts and boundaries may change, so candidates should check the latest official list before memorising numbers. Protected-area boundaries should be shown using an appropriately dated official map.

**Conclusion:** Conservation areas are effective only when legal designation is backed by management capacity, ecological monitoring and fair treatment of affected communities.

**Sources:** 47th BCS Written archive [S8]; Forest Department [S9]; Department of Environment [S13][S27].

## Q11. Discuss the economic and ecological importance of biodiversity. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Biodiversity includes variation within species, between species and among ecosystems. It supports food systems, fisheries, agriculture, medicine, tourism, genetic resources, pollination, soil formation, water regulation and resilience to environmental change. The Convention on Biological Diversity identifies conservation, sustainable use and fair benefit-sharing as linked objectives [S14].

In Bangladesh, biodiversity is found in mangroves, hill forests, rivers, wetlands, coastal waters, agricultural landscapes and marine ecosystems. The loss of species or habitat can affect fish breeding, natural pest control, soil quality, food security and the livelihoods of people who depend on ecosystems.

Major threats include habitat conversion, pollution, overexploitation, invasive species, unsustainable harvesting and climate change. Conservation should combine protected areas with habitat restoration, sustainable farming and fisheries, wildlife corridors where appropriate, pollution control, research and local stewardship. Biodiversity indicators should measure habitat quality and population trends, not only the number of protected sites.

Local communities need meaningful participation and fair benefits. Restrictions that impose high costs on poor households without alternatives can weaken public support for conservation.

**Conclusion:** Biodiversity is both an ecological foundation and a development asset. Protecting it requires coordinated land, water, agriculture, fisheries and climate policy.

**Sources:** Convention on Biological Diversity [S14]; Forest Department [S9]; UNESCO [S11].

## Q12. Analyse the importance of fisheries and interpret recent official fish-production data. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Fisheries provide animal protein, employment, export income, rural livelihoods and food security. Bangladesh’s fisheries include inland open-water capture, inland closed-water culture and marine fisheries. Sustainable planning must distinguish these sectors because their production systems, ecological pressures and management tools differ.

The Department of Fisheries reports total production of **50.18 lakh metric tonnes in 2023–24**, compared with 49.14 lakh tonnes in 2022–23, 47.59 lakh tonnes in 2021–22, 46.21 lakh tonnes in 2020–21 and 45.03 lakh tonnes in 2019–20 [S15]. In 2023–24, the source-wise figures were 14.11 lakh tonnes from inland open water, 29.78 lakh tonnes from inland closed water and 6.29 lakh tonnes from marine fisheries [S15].

These figures show growth in reported production over the period and the large contribution of aquaculture. They do **not**, by themselves, prove that natural fish stocks, water quality or biodiversity have improved. Production can rise while wild habitats face pressure.

Policy should protect breeding grounds, maintain wetland and river connectivity, improve water quality, promote responsible aquaculture, prevent harmful fishing practices, strengthen cold chains and support fishers during seasonal restrictions.

**Conclusion:** Fisheries success should be measured through production, nutrition, stock health, water quality and livelihoods together.

**Sources:** Department of Fisheries, fish-production statistics [S15]; *Yearbook of Fisheries Statistics of Bangladesh 2023–24* [S16].

## Q13. What measures are needed for sustainable fisheries and hilsa management? (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Sustainable fisheries management aims to maintain fish populations and habitats while supporting food security and the livelihoods of fishing communities. It requires both ecological controls and economic support.

For hilsa, important measures include protecting spawning and nursery areas; enforcing fishing restrictions and mesh-size rules; monitoring stock and catch trends; reducing illegal or destructive fishing; controlling pollution; and improving scientific data on migration, breeding and harvest. Seasonal restrictions can help protect reproductive cycles, but they can also impose immediate costs on fishers and their families.

Therefore, effective management should provide timely and accessible compensation, food support, alternative livelihood opportunities and fair access to markets. Community monitoring can improve compliance if fishers understand the purpose of restrictions and can influence implementation. Cold storage, transport and value addition can reduce post-harvest losses, while traceability can support responsible trade.

The Department of Fisheries reports hilsa production separately in its statistics, so any number quoted in an exam should include the reporting year and the official table used [S15]. A single annual increase or decline should not be interpreted without considering methods, effort, weather and stock conditions.

**Conclusion:** Fishery conservation works best when biological evidence, enforcement, local participation and livelihood protection are designed together.

**Sources:** Department of Fisheries [S15][S16]; 44th BCS Written archive [S17].

## Q14. What is the Blue Economy? Assess its prospects for Bangladesh. (20 marks)

**Model answer**

The Blue Economy refers to the sustainable use of ocean and coastal resources to support economic activity, livelihoods and well-being while maintaining the health of marine ecosystems. It is broader than extracting fish or minerals: it can include shipping, ports, marine research, tourism, coastal services, renewable energy and ecosystem restoration.

**Prospects:** Bangladesh’s Bay of Bengal coastline and marine jurisdiction support fisheries, transport and port services, coastal tourism, marine biodiversity, research and potential energy-related activity. A stronger marine value chain can create employment, improve food supply, support exports and encourage technical skills. Ports and shipping also connect Bangladesh to regional and global trade.

**Challenges:** overfishing, illegal fishing, marine pollution, habitat degradation, climate change, cyclones, limited research capacity, weak coordination, technology gaps and conflict among competing uses can undermine the benefits. Offshore extraction must be assessed carefully because revenue and energy security can come with pollution and ecosystem risks. Benefits may also be uneven if coastal communities lack access to finance, markets or decision-making.

**Way forward:** strengthen marine science and data systems; use ecosystem-based fisheries management; enforce pollution rules; improve port waste reception and spill preparedness; develop sustainable aquaculture and tourism; protect marine habitats; assess cumulative project impacts; train coastal workers; and make resource revenues and concessions transparent. International cooperation matters because marine ecosystems and pollution cross borders.

**Conclusion:** A successful Blue Economy should be judged by jobs, food security, public revenue and resilience **alongside** ecosystem condition and fair distribution of benefits.

**Sources:** BPSC [S1]; UNCLOS [S12]; 35th, 38th, 41st, 44th and 46th BCS archives [S6][S17][S18][S19][S20].

## Q15. Discuss the problems and prospects of marine-resource extraction and use in Bangladesh. (20 marks)

**Model answer**

Marine resources include living resources such as fish and other organisms, non-living resources such as minerals and potential energy resources, and ecosystem services that support coastal protection and tourism. The 46th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive reports a 20-mark question on problems and prospects in marine-resource extraction and use [S20]; the 44th BCS archive also includes a direct Blue Economy question [S17].

**Prospects:** marine fisheries can support nutrition and jobs; ports and shipping can strengthen trade; coastal tourism can generate local income; research may improve knowledge of offshore resources; and marine renewable-energy technologies may provide opportunities where technically and economically feasible.

**Problems:** limited scientific data and specialised technology; capital and infrastructure needs; illegal or unreported fishing; pollution and spill risk; habitat damage; safety challenges; weak monitoring; climate impacts; and possible exclusion of small-scale fishers from benefits. Natural-resource extraction may also create long-term environmental liabilities.

**Recommendations:** build marine research and monitoring capacity; enforce fishing and pollution regulations; protect spawning and sensitive habitats; require environmental and social assessments; develop skills and safe work standards; improve ports and waste management; provide coastal communities with fair access and consultation; and publish clear data on licences, revenue and environmental performance. Project selection should compare alternatives and include decommissioning or restoration costs where relevant.

**Conclusion:** Marine-resource use can contribute to development only when extraction remains within ecological limits and its economic benefits are transparent and fairly shared.

**Sources:** 44th and 46th BCS Written archives [S17][S20]; UNCLOS Part XII [S12].

## Q16. Introduce Bangladesh’s mineral resources and analyse the challenges of extraction. (10/20 marks)

**Model answer**

Mineral and geological resources support construction, energy, manufacturing and infrastructure. Bangladesh has identified resources such as natural gas, coal, limestone, hard rock, glass sand, silica sand, clay and other industrial minerals; their occurrence, reserve estimates and commercial viability differ by deposit. A resource occurrence should not automatically be described as an economically recoverable reserve.

The 37th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive includes a short note on Bangladesh’s mineral resources, while the 38th BCS archive asks about the problems and prospects of mineral extraction and industrial use [S21][S18].

Challenges include high exploration and extraction costs, uncertain reserve estimates, technology requirements, transport and processing infrastructure, land acquisition, water use, pollution, worker safety, community displacement and rehabilitation after extraction. Import dependence for some inputs can create supply and price risks, but domestic extraction is not always the best option if social and environmental costs are high.

A sound policy should improve geological surveys and transparent data; use independent feasibility studies; require environmental and social assessment; consult affected communities; enforce safety standards; publish licensing and revenue information; and budget for closure and restoration. Decisions should compare domestic extraction, recycling, imports and substitution.

**Conclusion:** Mineral policy should evaluate net public benefit—not merely the amount extracted or the number of projects approved.

**Sources:** 37th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive [S21]; 38th BCS Written archive [S18]; Geological Survey of Bangladesh [S22].

## Q17. Evaluate coal as an energy resource, considering economic and environmental trade-offs. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Coal can provide dispatchable electricity and support industrial activity, but it is a carbon-intensive fossil fuel with significant environmental and social costs. The 38th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive includes a question on coal’s importance as an energy resource [S18].

Potential benefits include reliable generation when fuel and plant operations are secure, reduced exposure to some fuel-supply risks and support for industrial demand. However, coal extraction and combustion can cause air pollution, greenhouse-gas emissions, ash-management problems, water use, land disturbance and health impacts. Mining can affect agricultural land and local communities, while power plants require long-lived infrastructure that may become costly as cleaner technologies develop.

Policy should compare full life-cycle costs, not only the plant-gate price of electricity. This includes fuel supply, grid reliability, emissions, health, water, land, waste, financing and decommissioning. Energy efficiency, improved grids, diversified supply and appropriately sited renewable energy can reduce demand for additional fossil-fuel capacity. Any coal project should have transparent assessment, strict emissions and waste controls, community consultation and a credible closure plan.

**Conclusion:** Coal’s role should be judged within a broader energy-security strategy that accounts for affordability, reliability, environmental damage and long-term climate commitments.

**Sources:** 38th BCS Written archive [S18]; Petrobangla annual reports [S23]; UN SDGs [S2].

## Q18. Discuss the role of natural gas in Bangladesh’s energy security and the need for efficient use. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Natural gas has supported electricity generation, industry, fertiliser production and other economic activities in Bangladesh. Its importance creates a need to manage supply, demand, infrastructure and environmental impacts carefully. The 41st BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive includes a short-note option on the proper use of gas resources [S19].

Key challenges include the difference between reported reserves and deliverable supply, uncertainty in exploration outcomes, infrastructure constraints, demand growth, price and import exposure, methane leakage and the need to maintain reliable distribution. Reserve and production figures change over time; candidates should use a dated Petrobangla report rather than memorising an undated estimate.

Efficient use requires reducing leakage, maintaining pipelines, improving industrial and power-plant efficiency, promoting conservation, monitoring consumption and using gas where it has high system value. Energy planning should compare gas with renewables, storage, demand management and other supply options, considering both reliability and cost. New exploration or infrastructure should be assessed against environmental risks and long-term demand scenarios.

**Conclusion:** Gas can contribute to energy security, but sustainable policy requires transparent reserve data, efficiency, diversification and environmental safeguards.

**Sources:** 41st BCS Written archive [S19]; Petrobangla annual reports [S23]; SREDA [S24].

## Q19. Evaluate the prospects and constraints of renewable energy in Bangladesh. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Renewable energy can diversify supply, reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels, support rural access and lower operational emissions. Solar energy is particularly relevant where sunlight, suitable land or rooftops, and reliable grid or storage arrangements are available. Other options—such as wind, biomass, small-scale hydro or waste-to-energy—must be assessed according to local resource quality and environmental effects.

Constraints include land scarcity, project finance, grid integration, storage costs, variability, import dependence for equipment, maintenance capacity and the need for predictable regulations. A project may be renewable yet still create land-use, biodiversity or waste problems if poorly planned.

Policy priorities should include rooftop solar, solar irrigation where viable, energy efficiency, improved transmission and distribution, battery or other storage where justified, transparent procurement, stable grid connection rules and end-of-life management for panels and batteries. Renewable-energy plans should not rely only on installed capacity; actual generation, system reliability, affordability and lifecycle impacts also matter.

**Conclusion:** Renewable energy is a central part of diversification, but it works best alongside efficiency, grid modernisation, storage, sound land planning and transparent regulation.

**Sources:** SREDA [S24]; BPSC [S1]; UN SDGs [S2].

## Q20. Explain the circular economy and its relevance to natural-resource management in Bangladesh. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

A circular economy aims to reduce waste and keep products and materials in productive use for as long as practical through durability, repair, reuse, remanufacturing, recycling and better product design. It differs from a linear model in which raw materials are extracted, used briefly and discarded.

For Bangladesh, circular approaches can reduce pressure on raw materials, lower landfill demand, improve material efficiency and create jobs in repair, recycling and resource recovery. Examples include separating municipal waste, recovering recyclable materials, composting suitable organic waste, improving industrial by-product use, repairing electronics, reducing food loss and improving construction-material efficiency.

However, recycling alone is not enough. Poorly managed recycling can expose workers to hazardous substances, contaminate waterways or shift costs to informal workers. Product design, safe working conditions, collection systems, standards and markets for recycled materials are essential. Producer responsibility, public procurement, reliable data and formal support for waste workers can improve outcomes.

**Conclusion:** A circular economy is a resource-efficiency strategy, not simply a waste campaign. It should be assessed through reduced virgin-material use, lower pollution, safe jobs and measurable waste prevention.

**Sources:** UN 2030 Agenda, especially SDG 12 [S2]; Department of Environment [S13].

## Q21. Analyse the effects of climate change on Bangladesh’s natural resources. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Climate change affects natural resources through rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, sea-level rise, salinity intrusion, stronger or more damaging coastal hazards, drought stress and changes in river and wetland systems. The scale and timing of impacts vary by region, so planning should use current climate and hydrological evidence.

Agriculture may face heat stress, irregular rainfall, salinity or waterlogging. Coastal freshwater and soils can be affected by saline intrusion. Forests and wetlands may experience shifts in habitat suitability, water quality or species distribution. Fisheries depend on water temperature, flow, breeding habitat and pollution conditions. Infrastructure and resource-dependent livelihoods may be exposed to cyclones, floods, erosion and prolonged drought.

Adaptation should combine early-warning systems, climate-resilient agriculture, efficient irrigation, restoration of mangroves and wetlands, improved drainage, flood-resilient infrastructure, groundwater monitoring, crop diversification and livelihood support. Nature-based solutions can complement—not replace—engineered protection and emergency preparedness.

Policies should monitor ecological and social outcomes and avoid maladaptation, such as infrastructure that transfers flood risk elsewhere or resource projects that increase exposure.

**Conclusion:** Climate adaptation is most effective when land, water, forests, agriculture and livelihoods are planned as an interconnected system.

**Sources:** BPSC [S1]; Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 atlas [S4]; UNFCCC [S25].

## Q22. What is Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)? Explain its importance and limitations. (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) is a structured process for identifying, predicting and evaluating the likely environmental and social effects of a proposed project before a decision is made. It helps compare alternatives, design mitigation and monitoring, and inform decision-makers and affected communities.

For natural-resource projects—such as mining, power generation, ports, roads, water-control structures or industrial sites—EIA can identify risks to land, water, air, biodiversity, livelihoods and public health. A good assessment considers project alternatives, baseline conditions, cumulative impacts, vulnerable groups, emergency risks and post-project restoration.

EIA can be weak if baseline data are poor, consultants face conflicts of interest, public consultation is superficial, mitigation is vague, or monitoring stops after approval. Project-by-project assessments may also miss cumulative impacts across a river basin or coastline. An EIA report does not itself guarantee compliance or sustainable outcomes.

To improve the process, publish accessible reports; ensure independent review; involve affected communities early; compare a genuine “no-project” option; set measurable mitigation conditions; disclose monitoring results; enforce penalties for non-compliance; and conduct post-approval audits.

**Conclusion:** EIA is useful when it changes decisions and improves accountability, not when it is treated as a formality.

**Sources:** Department of Environment [S13]; BPSC [S1]; Rio Declaration, Principle 10 [S26].

## Q23. Why is good governance essential for sustainable natural-resource management? (10 marks)

**Model answer**

Good governance makes resource decisions lawful, transparent, accountable, evidence-based and responsive to affected people. Natural resources often involve competing interests: public revenue, private investment, local livelihoods, conservation, energy supply and future generations. Without clear rules and oversight, benefits can be concentrated while environmental and social costs are transferred to others.

Key elements include clear institutional responsibility; open and reliable data; transparent licensing and procurement; enforcement of environmental and safety rules; meaningful public consultation; access to grievance mechanisms; independent monitoring; anti-corruption safeguards; and fair sharing of benefits. Scientific evidence must inform decisions, but local and Indigenous knowledge can also improve understanding of seasonal patterns, habitats and livelihood needs.

In Bangladesh, resource management often spans multiple institutions. Coordination is therefore needed among water, agriculture, fisheries, forests, energy, local government and environmental authorities. Joint indicators and public reporting can reduce duplication and reveal implementation gaps.

**Conclusion:** Good governance turns a policy promise into measurable practice. The test is whether resources remain productive, ecosystems recover, public funds are used transparently and communities experience fair outcomes.

**Sources:** BPSC [S1]; Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 [S4]; Department of Environment [S13].

## Q24. Discuss barriers to marginal farmers’ self-reliance and fair prices, and suggest remedies. (20 marks)

**Model answer**

Marginal farmers contribute to food production but often face limited land, small production volumes, high input costs, climate risks, weak bargaining power and restricted access to credit, storage and markets. The 46th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive reports a 20-mark question on obstacles to marginal farmers’ self-reliance and fair prices [S20].

**Major barriers:** fragmented landholdings; costly seeds, fertiliser, fuel and irrigation; limited access to extension services and climate information; crop losses from floods, droughts, salinity and pests; lack of storage and cold chains; dependence on intermediaries; limited price information; and difficulty meeting quality standards or accessing formal finance. Women farmers and tenant farmers may face additional constraints where ownership records or services are not designed for their needs.

**Remedies:** expand accessible agricultural extension and soil testing; improve irrigation efficiency and climate-resilient varieties; strengthen farmer cooperatives and producer organisations; invest in local storage, grading, transport and cold chains; publish market-price information; improve access to affordable credit and crop insurance where feasible; develop transparent procurement and fair competition; reduce post-harvest loss; and ensure women and tenant farmers can access services. Public support should be monitored to avoid capture by better-connected producers.

**Conclusion:** Fair prices require more than a support announcement. Farmers need productive assets, market access, bargaining power, risk protection and transparent value chains.

**Sources:** 46th BCS Written archive [S20]; Department of Fisheries and BBS data portals where relevant [S5][S15].

## Q25. Which sectors should Bangladesh prioritise for sustainable development by using natural resources, human resources, geography and global economic trends? (20 marks)

**Model answer**

Sustainable development priorities should reflect Bangladesh’s resource base, population, geographic position, climate exposure and changing global markets. The 47th BCS Bangladesh Affairs archive reports a question asking candidates to identify priority sectors using natural resources, human resources, geography and global economic trends [S8].

**First, agriculture and food systems:** protect productive land, improve soil and water management, strengthen research, reduce post-harvest loss and support farmers’ bargaining power.

**Second, water and climate resilience:** integrate river-basin planning, drainage, wetland protection, early warning, salinity management and climate-resilient infrastructure.

**Third, Blue Economy:** develop sustainable fisheries, ports, shipping, marine research and coastal tourism while enforcing pollution controls and protecting marine habitats.

**Fourth, forests and biodiversity:** restore degraded ecosystems, improve protected-area management and align local livelihoods with conservation.

**Fifth, energy and resource efficiency:** diversify supply, expand viable renewables, improve grid performance, reduce waste and manage fossil fuels responsibly.

**Sixth, skills and technology:** train workers in marine science, agriculture, environmental monitoring, renewable energy, modern manufacturing and circular-economy activities.

**Seventh, transparent governance:** improve data, land-use planning, EIA, licensing, inter-agency coordination and public accountability.

These priorities should be ranked through feasibility, employment potential, ecological limits, distributional effects, import exposure and long-term resilience—not political visibility alone.

**Conclusion:** Bangladesh’s advantage will come from raising the value and productivity of its resources while protecting the ecosystems and communities on which future growth depends.

**Sources:** 47th BCS Written archive [S8]; BPSC [S1]; Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100 [S4]; UN SDGs [S2].
