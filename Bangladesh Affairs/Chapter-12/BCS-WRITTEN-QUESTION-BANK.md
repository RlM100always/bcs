# Chapter 12 — 25 High-priority BCS Written Questions with Model Answers
## Communication, ICT, Media, RTI and E-Governance

## How to write these answers
- **5 marks:** definition + 3–4 key points + one Bangladesh example + short conclusion.
- **10 marks:** introduction + framework/evidence + benefits and limitations + recommendations.
- **15–20 marks:** define the problem, analyse causes and impacts, cite law/data, evaluate trade-offs, give implementable recommendations, and end with a balanced judgement.

## Q01. Define communication and explain the role of feedback. (5 marks)

**Model answer:** Communication is the process through which a sender encodes a message and transmits it through a channel to a receiver, who interprets it in a particular context. Feedback tells the sender whether the message was understood and whether further clarification is needed. The process may be affected by noise, language barriers, low literacy, inaccessible formats or distrust. In public administration, a notice alone is not effective communication if citizens cannot understand it or ask questions. Two-way communication uses public consultation, help desks, community radio, accessible websites and complaint systems to test understanding. **Conclusion:** Communication is effective when information is accurate, understandable, accessible and open to feedback.

**Source line:** BPSC, *Syllabus for BCS (Written) Examination*, Bangladesh Affairs, Unit 12.

## Q02. How can development communication contribute to Bangladesh's socio-economic development? (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Development communication uses planned, audience-sensitive communication to help people understand choices, access services and participate in change. In Bangladesh it can support public health, disaster preparedness, agriculture, education, financial literacy and social protection. First, clear messages can explain vaccination, safe water, cyclone warnings or crop disease. Second, two-way communication allows local knowledge and concerns to reach service providers. Third, community radio, mobile messages and local-language materials can reach people who have limited access to national media. Fourth, communication can make public programmes more transparent by publishing eligibility, fees, deadlines and complaint routes. Yet one-way publicity, inaccessible language and unverified messages can reduce trust. Programmes should pre-test messages, use multiple channels, protect personal data and measure whether people understood and used the information—not merely count broadcasts or views.

**Source line:** BPSC Written Syllabus, Unit 12; UN SDG 16.6–16.10.

## Q03. Analyse the contribution of ICT to Bangladesh's development. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Information and Communication Technology (ICT) supports the collection, processing, storage and exchange of information. It can reduce transaction costs and expand access to education, health advice, agricultural information, markets, banking and public services. Digital platforms can help businesses reach customers, citizens track applications and agencies exchange records. Bangladesh's National Portal, Digital Centres and D-Nothi illustrate different parts of this service ecosystem. However, ICT does not automatically produce development. Weak connectivity, device and data costs, low digital literacy, inaccessible design, cybersecurity risks and poor underlying procedures may exclude citizens or reproduce existing inequalities. The state should invest in reliable networks, skills, Bengali-language content, accessible design, secure systems and assisted/offline channels. Performance should be judged by completed services, reduced time and cost, reliability, grievance resolution and inclusion—not by the number of websites launched.

**Sources:** [ICT Division — National ICT Policy 2018](https://ictd.gov.bd/pages/policies); [National Digital Centre portal](https://digitalcentre.gov.bd/); [Bangladesh National Portal](https://bangladesh.gov.bd/).

## Q04. Explain the opportunities and limits of Bangladesh's digital transformation. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Digital transformation is not simply converting paper forms into online forms; it redesigns services, records and decision processes around users and reliable data. Opportunities include faster applications, easier payments, better status tracking, remote access, data-informed planning and improved coordination among offices. It may lower travel costs for rural citizens and help small firms use digital markets. Its limits include uneven connectivity, unreliable power, fragmented databases, duplicate data entry, unclear accountability, privacy breaches and exclusion of people without devices or skills. A digital service may still be inefficient if the underlying approval chain is unnecessarily complex. Bangladesh should simplify procedures before digitising them, adopt interoperable standards, publish service standards, conduct security and accessibility testing, and retain assisted service options. A public dashboard should report service completion time, failure rates, cost, uptime and user complaints.

**Source line:** ICT Division, National ICT Policy 2018; a2i, Government Service Portal.

## Q05. What is the digital divide? Discuss its causes and remedies in Bangladesh. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** The digital divide is unequal access to, capability to use, and benefit from digital technologies. It is not limited to whether a mobile signal exists. The divide can arise from rural–urban infrastructure gaps, device and data costs, gendered control of devices, disability barriers, age, language, literacy and lack of confidence. People may have internet access but still be unable to complete a digital government service. Remedies include affordable broadband, reliable electricity, public access points, digital-skills training, Bengali and local-language content, accessible interfaces, assistive technology and privacy-safe support. Digital Centres can help people who need assisted access, while online services should keep clear help desks and alternative routes. Results should be disaggregated by geography, gender, disability and income where lawful. **Conclusion:** Inclusion requires access, skills, affordability, safety and meaningful outcomes together.

**Sources:** [Digital Centre portal](https://digitalcentre.gov.bd/); [UN SDG 10](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal10).

## Q06. Distinguish e-government from e-governance. (5 marks)

**Model answer:** **E-government** commonly refers to the use of digital tools to deliver public services and manage administrative processes—for example, online applications, digital payments and electronic files. **E-governance** is broader: it includes how technology changes transparency, participation, accountability, coordination and the relationship between citizens and public institutions. An online form is an e-government tool; a system that publishes service standards, records decisions, permits tracking and offers an effective appeal mechanism contributes to e-governance. The distinction is not absolute, and authors sometimes use the terms interchangeably. In an exam, define the terms and then evaluate whether the technology improves fairness, responsiveness and public value. **Conclusion:** Digital tools matter, but institutional rules and accountability determine whether they support good governance.

**Sources:** BPSC Written Syllabus, Unit 12; UN SDG 16.

## Q07. Evaluate the impact of e-governance on public service delivery in Bangladesh. (20 marks)

**Model answer:** E-governance can make public services more accessible, traceable and efficient when it is combined with process reform and accountability. Online applications and payments may reduce travel, queues and opportunities for informal payments. Digital records can make files easier to trace; shared systems can reduce repeated submissions; service portals can publish requirements and timelines. Bangladesh's Digital Centres provide an assisted channel for citizens who cannot comfortably navigate online services alone. The official Digital Centre portal currently reports its own operational statistics; these should be cited with the date consulted because the dashboard changes. Yet e-governance can also create exclusion through poor connectivity, confusing interfaces, system downtime, data mismatch, cyber fraud and inaccessible complaint routes. Digitising a discretionary or opaque process may simply move the same problem online. Priorities should be: simplify procedures, publish standards and fees, ensure interoperability, protect personal data, test accessibility, keep offline/assisted options, and audit service outcomes. Measure completion time, cost, rejection/error rates, uptime, user satisfaction and grievance resolution. **Conclusion:** E-governance is successful when citizens experience a demonstrable improvement in service quality and equal treatment.

**Archive-reported PYQ link:** 45th BCS Written, Bangladesh Affairs, Question 32 (20 marks; wording should be checked against an original BPSC scan). [Question archive](https://bcsexam.org/written-questions/45th-bcs-written).

## Q08. What is the Right to Information (RTI) and what is its legal basis in Bangladesh? (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Right to Information is the legal entitlement to seek information held by public authorities, subject to the law's scope and lawful exceptions. Bangladesh's *Right to Information Act, 2009* provides the statutory framework. Section 4 states the citizen's right to obtain information from an authority, subject to the Act. Section 5 requires authorities to prepare catalogues and indexes of their information and preserve it properly. These provisions show that RTI depends both on a right to request information and on sound record-keeping. RTI can help citizens understand public spending, service eligibility, decisions and project progress, and can support journalism and research. It is not an unlimited right to every personal, confidential or legally exempt record; requests and disclosure must follow the current law and rules. **Conclusion:** RTI is meaningful when the law, records management and review mechanisms work in practice.

**Primary legal source:** [Laws of Bangladesh — Right to Information Act, 2009](https://bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd/act-print-1011.html).

## Q09. Differentiate proactive disclosure from request-based disclosure. (5 marks)

**Model answer:** **Proactive disclosure** means that an authority publishes important information without waiting for an individual request—for example, its functions, service requirements, fees, budgets, decisions or procurement information where disclosure is lawful. **Request-based disclosure** occurs when a citizen asks an authority for a particular record through the procedure prescribed by law. Proactive disclosure reduces repetitive requests and helps citizens know what services exist. Request-based disclosure is important when the information a person needs is not already published. Both require accurate records, clear responsibility, accessible formats and timely updating. Personal information and legally protected material must be handled according to applicable law. **Conclusion:** A strong RTI system combines routine public disclosure with an effective individual-request process.

**Sources:** RTI Act, 2009, sections 4–5; [Information Commission Bangladesh](https://infocom.gov.bd/).

## Q10. How does RTI strengthen transparency and accountability? (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Transparency makes public decisions and records visible; accountability requires officials to explain decisions and face appropriate review or consequences. RTI supports both by giving citizens a legal route to obtain information. Journalists can use records to verify public claims; researchers can assess programmes; citizens can check service requirements, spending or project progress; civil society can compare promises with results. Information is more useful when it is timely, comprehensible and supported by reliable records. RTI alone cannot prevent corruption: weak record-keeping, delayed responses, low awareness, broad claims of exemption or ineffective review may blunt its impact. Authorities should appoint and train responsible officers, maintain catalogues and indexes, publish frequently requested information, preserve records and monitor response times. The Information Commission and applicable statutory review routes should be accessible. **Conclusion:** Information becomes accountability when it can be verified, discussed and connected to an effective remedy.

**Sources:** RTI Act, 2009; UN SDG 16.6 and 16.10.

## Q11. Identify the main barriers to effective RTI implementation and recommend reforms. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Common implementation barriers include poor record management, limited awareness, unclear responsibility, delayed processing, inaccessible formats, low staff capacity and uncertainty about lawful exemptions. Citizens may not know which authority holds a record or how to pursue a complaint. Digital systems can help index and retrieve records, but scanning documents without classification or retention rules is not enough. Reforms should include: appointing trained information officers; creating searchable catalogues; adopting consistent retention and archiving schedules; proactively publishing frequently requested records; explaining procedures in plain Bengali and accessible formats; monitoring deadlines; recording reasons for refusal; and ensuring review mechanisms are understandable. Privacy should be protected by disclosing only what the law permits and redacting personal details where appropriate. Results should be reported through response-time and resolution indicators. **Conclusion:** RTI requires a records culture and accountable institutions, not just a statute.

**Sources:** RTI Act, 2009, sections 4–5; Information Commission Bangladesh.

## Q12. Why is the media called a watchdog of democracy? (10 marks)

**Model answer:** The media acts as a democratic watchdog when it independently gathers evidence, scrutinises the use of power and informs the public about decisions that affect them. Investigative reporting can expose corruption, maladministration and rights violations. News media also help citizens compare policy choices, understand budgets and emergencies, and bring neglected communities into public debate. The watchdog role depends on access to information, editorial independence, source verification, protection of journalists and the ability to correct mistakes. It is weakened by political or commercial pressure, conflicts of interest, sensationalism and misinformation. Media freedom must coexist with professional standards, privacy, fair reporting and lawful limits. A healthy media environment encourages criticism based on evidence rather than suppressing disagreement or spreading unverified allegations. **Conclusion:** A free, plural and responsible media strengthens accountability by making public-interest information available and testable.

**Sources:** Constitution of Bangladesh, Article 39; UDHR Article 19; UN SDG 16.10.

## Q13. Discuss the balance between media freedom and media responsibility. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Media freedom protects the ability to investigate, criticise and communicate information without improper interference. In Bangladesh, Article 39 of the Constitution recognises freedom of thought and conscience, speech and expression, and freedom of the press subject to the constitutional text and applicable restrictions. Responsibility means verifying claims, distinguishing fact from opinion, correcting errors, avoiding discriminatory language, respecting privacy and making conflicts of interest transparent. These responsibilities should be implemented through professional ethics, transparent correction practices, media literacy and independent accountability—not through vague rules that chill legitimate scrutiny. Journalists should use primary documents, seek responses from affected parties and disclose uncertainty. Audiences should be able to identify sponsored content and report errors. **Conclusion:** Freedom enables scrutiny; ethics and credible correction sustain the trust on which journalism depends.

**Sources:** [Constitution of Bangladesh](https://bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd/act-367.html), Article 39; [UN UDHR](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights), Article 19.

## Q14. Differentiate misinformation and disinformation and propose remedies. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** **Misinformation** is false or misleading information shared without necessarily intending to deceive; **disinformation** is false or misleading content spread deliberately to deceive or manipulate. Both can damage public health, elections, communal relations and trust in institutions. Remedies should not rely on censorship alone. Public authorities should publish timely, verifiable updates; media organisations should verify original sources and images; platforms should improve reporting and transparency; schools and communities should teach media literacy; and independent fact-checkers should explain evidence in accessible language. Corrections should reach the same audiences as the original claim. During emergencies, trusted local communicators and clear uncertainty statements matter. Responses must protect legitimate debate and avoid labelling all criticism as misinformation. **Conclusion:** A resilient information environment combines verification, transparency, media literacy, timely correction and rights-respecting governance.

**Sources:** UN SDG 16; UDHR Article 19; BPSC Unit 12.

## Q15. Evaluate social media's role in public opinion and citizen participation. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Social media lowers the cost of publishing and sharing information, allows citizens to organise, gives public institutions a direct communication channel and can amplify voices ignored by traditional media. It can support emergency alerts, civic campaigns and rapid feedback on services. However, algorithmic amplification, fake accounts, harassment, privacy violations, echo chambers and viral falsehoods can distort debate. A popular post is not necessarily reliable evidence. Public institutions should publish source documents, explain decisions, correct mistakes and offer accessible complaint routes. Journalists and users should check dates, original context and independent corroboration before sharing claims. Digital literacy and transparent platform practices can reduce harm. **Conclusion:** Social media expands participation, but the quality of public opinion depends on evidence, pluralism, safety and accountability.

**Sources:** UDHR Article 19; UN SDG 16.10.

## Q16. Explain the strategic importance of submarine cables and digital infrastructure to Bangladesh. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** International connectivity is a foundation for digital services, cloud applications, trade, education, communication and data-intensive business. Submarine cables carry international traffic to landing stations and connect through backhaul fibre and domestic networks to internet gateways, service providers and users. Bangladesh Submarine Cables PLC identifies Cox's Bazar and Kuakata as landing-station locations and describes its participation in international cable systems including SMW4, SMW5 and SMW6. These details should be checked against current company information before an exam, and a historical cable-route map must not be treated as a complete current map. Resilience requires route diversity, repair readiness, domestic backhaul, reliable power, secure network management and alternative routes. **Conclusion:** Connectivity is strategic infrastructure; redundancy and resilience matter as much as nominal capacity.

**Sources:** [BSCPLC service information](https://bscplc.portal.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/6922de55933eb65569e19f01); [BTRC statistics](https://btrc.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/6922e009933eb65569e2524c).

## Q17. What do internet subscription statistics tell us—and what do they not tell us? (5 marks)

**Model answer:** BTRC's published table reported 130.07 million total internet subscriptions in February 2025, 128.27 million in February 2026 and 131.42 million in April 2026. These are subscription counts, not a headcount of unique individuals. One person may hold multiple SIMs or connections, while some subscriptions may be shared or not regularly used. Subscription totals therefore should not be equated automatically with unique internet users, quality of access, affordability or meaningful use. For a stronger analysis, combine subscription data with household access, speed, coverage, affordability, device ownership, gender and regional indicators. Always state the reference month and the exact indicator. **Conclusion:** A statistic is useful only when its unit, denominator and limitations are clear.

**Source:** [BTRC — Internet Subscribers](https://btrc.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/6922e0a3933eb65569e27f59), monthly table; selected observations cited as published in 2025–2026.

## Q18. Explain how Digital Centres can support inclusive e-governance. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Digital Centres provide an assisted local channel through which citizens can access digital government and private services with help from trained entrepreneurs. This is important for people who lack devices, confidence, digital skills or reliable connectivity. Services may include applications, certificates, utility payments, information, training and other locally available services. The official Digital Centre portal currently displays operational statistics and a service portfolio; because the dashboard changes, quote its figures only with the consultation date. Benefits can include less travel, lower transaction costs and easier access for rural citizens, women and older people. Risks include unofficial charges, variable service quality, data privacy problems and uneven centre activity. Reforms should publish fees, issue receipts, train entrepreneurs, monitor completion and complaint rates, and protect citizen data. **Conclusion:** Assisted access complements—not replaces—secure, accessible online services and accountable public authorities.

**Sources:** [Official Digital Centre portal](https://digitalcentre.gov.bd/); [Bangladesh National Portal — Digital Centre](https://bangladesh.gov.bd/pages/static-pages/69a55ba386514399668e4e65).

## Q19. Why are privacy and cybersecurity essential in public digital services? (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Public digital services may process identity, address, financial, health and family information. Weak access controls, excessive data collection or insecure storage can expose citizens to fraud, discrimination and loss of trust. Cybersecurity protects confidentiality, integrity and availability; privacy governs whether personal data is collected, used, shared and retained appropriately. Good practice includes data minimisation, clear lawful purpose, least-privilege access, multifactor authentication where appropriate, encryption, secure backups, audit logs, staff training, incident response and secure deletion. Procurement should include security requirements and independent testing. Services should collect only what is needed and explain how data is used. Security incidents require containment, evidence preservation, recovery and notification where law or policy requires it. **Conclusion:** Privacy and security must be built into design and operations, not added after a breach.

**Source line:** National ICT Policy 2018; ICT Division policy and data-centre guidance; applicable current laws and rules.

## Q20. Discuss the potential and risks of AI in government services. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** Artificial intelligence can assist with document classification, translation, citizen query routing, fraud-risk flags and summarising records. It may improve speed, but its outputs can be wrong, biased, difficult to explain or based on poor-quality data. In high-impact areas such as benefits, licensing, enforcement or eligibility, an automated recommendation should not become an unreviewable final decision. Authorities should define a lawful purpose, test data quality, assess bias and security, document system limits, retain audit trails and provide meaningful human review and appeal. Citizens should know when AI materially affects a decision and how to seek correction. Procurement should address vendor accountability, data use and system updates. **Conclusion:** AI can assist public servants, but legal responsibility, fairness and redress must remain with accountable institutions.

**Source line:** UN SDG 16; National ICT Policy 2018; BPSC Unit 12.

## Q21. Explain G2C, G2B, G2G and G2E with examples. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** **G2C (Government to Citizen)** includes online applications, certificates, benefits and service tracking. **G2B (Government to Business)** includes electronic procurement, tax filing, licensing and regulatory compliance. **G2G (Government to Government)** includes secure data exchange, interoperable records and inter-agency workflows. **G2E (Government to Employee)** includes payroll, leave, training and human-resource systems. The categories are useful for organising an answer, but real systems often connect several categories. Their success depends on reliable identity management, common data standards, accessibility, cybersecurity, clear ownership and complaint mechanisms. Interoperability should not mean unlimited data sharing; each exchange needs a lawful purpose and appropriate access controls. **Conclusion:** The models describe who interacts with whom, while good governance depends on how safely and fairly the interaction works.

**Source line:** BPSC Written Syllabus, Unit 12; National ICT Policy 2018.

## Q22. Why is e-tendering important for public procurement, and what safeguards are needed? (10 marks)

**Model answer:** E-tendering uses electronic systems to publish tender notices, distribute documents, receive bids and maintain procurement records. It can widen access to information, reduce paperwork, create a time-stamped audit trail and make some stages easier to monitor. The 36th BCS Written Bangladesh Affairs archive includes a short question on the meaning and introduction of e-tendering, while the current procurement framework and dates should be verified from official procurement authorities before quoting. Technology alone cannot eliminate collusion, tailored specifications or conflicts of interest. Safeguards include transparent criteria, secure bid submission, equal access to notices, documented evaluation, conflict-of-interest controls, independent audit and effective complaints. **Conclusion:** E-tendering supports integrity when paired with competitive rules, competent officials and credible enforcement.

**Source:** [36th BCS Written archive](https://bcsexam.org/written-questions/36th-bcs-written); verify procurement-law details with the official procurement authority.

## Q23. How can Bangladesh ensure accessible digital services for women, older people and persons with disabilities? (15 marks)

**Model answer:** Inclusive digital services must be designed around different needs rather than assuming every user has a recent smartphone, high literacy, fast internet and full vision or hearing. Key measures include screen-reader compatibility, keyboard navigation, captions, readable contrast, simple Bengali, low-bandwidth pages, clear error messages and forms that work on mobile devices. Assisted access through Digital Centres and help desks can support people who need human help. Training should include women and older people, while public devices and service points should be safe and accessible. Services must protect personal information and avoid forcing users to disclose sensitive details to intermediaries unnecessarily. Governments should test with users with disabilities and publish accessibility standards. Indicators should include task completion and error rates across user groups. **Conclusion:** Accessibility is a service-quality and equal-citizenship requirement, not a decorative feature.

**Sources:** Bangladesh National Portal, Digital Centre resources; UN SDG 10 and 16.

## Q24. Explain how ICT can improve education while avoiding a new learning divide. (10 marks)

**Model answer:** ICT can expand access to learning materials, support teacher development, enable remote lessons, improve communication with families and provide digital assessment or administrative tools. Yet devices and internet access alone do not guarantee learning. Students need trained teachers, relevant curriculum, accessible Bengali content, feedback and a safe learning environment. The digital divide can widen when some learners have personal devices and quiet study spaces while others depend on shared phones or unstable connections. A balanced strategy combines digital resources with classroom teaching, printed alternatives where needed, teacher support, child protection, privacy and accessible formats. Learning outcomes should be measured through understanding and skills rather than platform logins alone. **Conclusion:** Technology strengthens education when it serves pedagogy and inclusion rather than substituting for teachers or equal access.

**Archive-reported connection:** 47th BCS Written Bangladesh Affairs, Question 31, on the role of practical curricula, teaching, teacher training and technology in education. [Question archive](https://bcsexam.org/written-questions/47th-bcs-written).

## Q25. How can ICT, media freedom, RTI and e-governance reinforce one another? (20 marks)

**Model answer:** These four elements are mutually reinforcing but serve different functions. ICT enables the storage, processing and delivery of information. RTI provides a legal route to seek public records and depends on reliable records management. The media verifies information, investigates public-interest issues and communicates findings to citizens. E-governance uses technology to make public services and administrative processes more accessible, traceable and responsive. Together they can reduce information gaps and support participation. However, digitalisation without privacy safeguards can expose personal data; RTI without good records may be ineffective; media without source verification can spread falsehoods; and portals without appeal routes may digitise unfair procedures. An integrated policy should ensure proactive disclosure, searchable records, independent journalism, corrections, accessible services, secure data exchange, digital literacy and effective complaints. Progress should be measured through service completion, response times, correction quality, public access and resolution of grievances. **Conclusion:** Technology is an enabling infrastructure; law, professional standards and accountable institutions convert it into public value.

**Sources:** [BPSC syllabus](https://bpsc.portal.gov.bd/sites/default/files/files/bpsc.portal.gov.bd/page/732e9da5_9514_4252_9a33_119f8c000cc5/SYLLABUS%20FOR%20BCS%20%28WRITTEN%29%20EXAMINATION.pdf); [RTI Act, 2009](https://bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd/act-print-1011.html); [UN SDG 16](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal16).

## One-page revision framework

| Theme | Must include | Common mistake to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback, noise | treating communication as one-way publicity |
| ICT | access, capability, use, development outcomes | assuming technology automatically creates development |
| Media | watchdog, pluralism, verification, ethics, corrections | equating freedom with absence of responsibility |
| RTI | Act 2009, sections 4–5, records, lawful exceptions, review | claiming access to every record without exception |
| E-governance | service redesign, inclusion, interoperability, accountability | counting websites instead of measuring outcomes |
| Digital divide | affordability, rurality, gender, disability, skills | treating coverage as meaningful access |
| Cybersecurity/privacy | data minimisation, access control, encryption, response | collecting unnecessary personal data |
| AI | purpose, quality, bias audit, human review, appeal | accepting automated output without review |
