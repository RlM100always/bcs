# Chapter 15 — 25 BCS Written Model Questions with Answers
## Gender Issues and Development in Bangladesh

> **How to use:** These are high-priority syllabus-based practice questions, not claimed to be verbatim past questions. For 5 marks write a definition plus 3–4 points; for 10 marks add Bangladesh context and examples; for 20 marks use the analytical structure and recommendations.

## A. Core concepts

### 1. Define gender equality and distinguish it from gender equity.
**Answer:** Gender equality means that people of all genders enjoy equal rights, responsibilities, opportunities and protection. Gender equity refers to fair measures that address different starting points and structural disadvantages so that equality becomes meaningful. Equality is the desired condition; equity can be a means to achieve it. For example, equal access to education may require safe transport, sanitation, stipends and targeted support for disadvantaged girls. Equity must be evidence-based and consistent with rights, not a justification for permanent unequal treatment.

### 2. Distinguish sex, gender, gender roles and gender stereotypes.
**Answer:** Sex refers to biological characteristics; gender describes socially constructed roles, expectations and power relations. Gender roles are expectations about who performs paid work, care or leadership. Gender stereotypes are generalised assumptions—for example, that leadership is naturally male or care work is exclusively female. Such stereotypes can restrict education, occupation, mobility and decision-making. Policy should address discriminatory institutions and behaviour while respecting individual dignity and diversity.

### 3. What is women’s empowerment?
**Answer:** Women’s empowerment is the expansion of women’s ability to make strategic life choices and exercise control over resources and decisions. Its dimensions include economic resources, education and skills, bodily autonomy and safety, political voice, legal awareness, and household/community decision-making. Employment alone does not guarantee empowerment if work is unsafe, poorly paid or unpaid care remains unequal. Outcomes should be assessed through agency, control over income/assets, freedom from violence and meaningful participation.

### 4. Explain the relationship between gender equality and sustainable development.
**Answer:** Gender equality is both a human-rights obligation and a development condition. When girls complete education, women access decent work and all people participate in decisions, societies can improve productivity, health, poverty reduction and institutional legitimacy. Inequality wastes human capability and can perpetuate intergenerational poverty. SDG 5 addresses equality and empowerment, while gender equality also affects education, health, decent work, climate resilience and reduced inequality. Progress requires rights-based law, public services, financing and measurable accountability.

### 5. What is intersectionality in gender analysis?
**Answer:** Intersectionality examines how gender interacts with poverty, disability, age, location, ethnicity, displacement and other factors to shape disadvantage. A rural woman with a disability may face barriers different from those experienced by an urban professional woman. One-size-fits-all programmes can miss these differences. Collecting ethically disaggregated data, consulting affected groups and making services accessible help policy respond to intersecting barriers without treating any group as homogeneous.

## B. Bangladesh context and institutions

### 6. Assess progress and remaining challenges in girls’ education in Bangladesh.
**Answer:** Bangladesh has expanded girls’ access to primary and secondary education through public investment, stipends and community-level initiatives. Yet access does not automatically ensure completion, learning quality or transition to higher education and decent work. Poverty, child marriage, safety concerns, care responsibilities, disability-related barriers and digital inequality can interrupt learning. Priorities include safe schools and transport, menstrual-health facilities, quality teaching, inclusive classrooms, prevention of harassment, second-chance learning and reliable data disaggregated by sex, location and disability.

### 7. Discuss women’s participation in Bangladesh’s labour market.
**Answer:** Women contribute to ready-made garments, agriculture, services, entrepreneurship and unpaid family work. Their participation can increase household income, export capacity and economic resilience. However, occupational segregation, wage gaps, informal employment, harassment, unsafe transport, limited childcare, maternity-related discrimination and unpaid care constrain the quality of participation. Policy should promote decent work, equal pay for work of equal value, safe workplaces, social protection, skills, childcare, transport and women’s access to credit, technology and markets. Measure job quality as well as employment rates.

### 8. Why is unpaid care work a gender-development issue?
**Answer:** Cooking, cleaning, caring for children, older people and sick family members sustain the economy but are often uncounted and unequally distributed. Excessive unpaid care creates time poverty and reduces opportunities for education, paid work, rest and civic participation. Policy can recognise, reduce and redistribute care through public childcare, eldercare, water and energy infrastructure, social protection, flexible decent work and men’s participation. Time-use surveys help reveal the scale and distribution of this work.

### 9. Explain the effects of child marriage on development.
**Answer:** Child marriage can interrupt education, restrict autonomy, increase early-pregnancy health risks and expose girls to unequal power relations and violence. It may reinforce poverty across generations by reducing skills and future earnings. Prevention requires enforcement of applicable law, birth registration, school retention, social protection for poor households, adolescent-friendly health services, community engagement and safe reporting. Programmes should address the economic and social drivers and avoid blaming girls or families without examining structural pressures.

### 10. Analyse gender-based violence as a public policy issue.
**Answer:** Gender-based violence is a human-rights violation and a barrier to health, education, employment and public participation. It includes intimate-partner violence, sexual harassment, child abuse, trafficking and online abuse. Effective responses combine prevention, accessible reporting, survivor-centred health and psychosocial services, safe shelters, legal aid, trained investigators, fair trials and protection from retaliation. Prevention requires education, community engagement and changing harmful norms. Use BBS’s Violence Against Women Survey 2024 and UNFPA’s report for precisely defined indicators; do not mix lifetime and past-year prevalence.

### 11. Explain the importance of gender-responsive budgeting.
**Answer:** Gender-responsive budgeting assesses how public revenue, expenditure and services affect different genders. It does not mean creating a separate budget for women or dividing every allocation equally. It links policy goals to resources, output indicators and outcomes. A sound process identifies unequal needs, consults affected communities, allocates adequate funds, tracks execution and evaluates impact. Published budget tags alone do not prove equality; expenditure quality and measurable changes in access, safety and agency matter.

### 12. Discuss women’s political participation and leadership.
**Answer:** Political participation includes voting, candidacy, party decision-making, elected office, public administration and community leadership. Women’s representation can widen policy perspectives, but numerical presence alone does not guarantee influence. Barriers include unequal campaign resources, political violence, harassment, party gatekeeping, care burdens and social stereotypes. Measures include fair nomination processes, transparent party rules, safe political participation, leadership training, access to finance, accountability and support for women’s substantive influence in committees and policy decisions.

### 13. Explain the role of law and the Constitution in gender equality.
**Answer:** Constitutional guarantees of equality before law and non-discrimination provide a foundational framework, alongside protections relevant to public participation. Specific legislation and policies address violence, child marriage, labour conditions and other areas. Law is effective only when people know their rights and can access police, courts, legal aid and protective services. Implementation must be consistent, timely and fair. In an exam, cite the exact constitutional article or statute and verify its current text, amendments and scope from official sources.

### 14. What is the role of CEDAW in Bangladesh’s gender policy?
**Answer:** The Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women establishes international obligations to eliminate discrimination in law and practice across public and private life. It provides a framework for reviewing legislation, education, employment, health, political participation and harmful stereotypes. International commitments do not implement themselves: domestic laws, institutional capacity, resources, monitoring and remedies are needed. When discussing Bangladesh’s position, verify current ratification status and any reservations from official UN treaty records.

### 15. How can digital inclusion advance gender equality?
**Answer:** Digital access can expand education, financial services, entrepreneurship, information, public services and civic participation. However, device affordability, connectivity, digital literacy, privacy risks, online harassment and restrictive household norms may widen gaps. Solutions include affordable access, practical skills, accessible design, online safety education, reliable reporting channels and protection of personal data. Measure not only phone ownership but meaningful use, skills, safety and control over devices and accounts.

## C. Critical and applied questions

### 16. Analyse the gender dimensions of climate change and disasters in Bangladesh.
**Answer:** Climate hazards affect livelihoods, health, mobility and access to services, but impacts differ according to social roles, assets, location and decision-making power. Women may face specific safety, sanitation, care and livelihood constraints during displacement or evacuation, while women-led groups also contribute important local knowledge and resilience. Gender-responsive preparedness includes sex- and disability-disaggregated risk data, inclusive early warning, safe shelters, accessible sanitation, participation in local committees and equitable recovery finance. Avoid portraying women only as victims.

### 17. How can Bangladesh reduce the gender pay and opportunity gap?
**Answer:** Key measures include transparent pay systems, enforcement against discrimination, skills development, access to high-productivity occupations, safe workplaces, affordable childcare, transport, social protection and equal access to finance and technology. Employers should track recruitment, promotion, retention and pay by sex while protecting privacy. Public procurement and labour inspection can support compliance. Evaluation should distinguish differences in hourly pay, monthly earnings, occupation and working hours rather than citing a single unexplained “pay gap.”

### 18. Explain the importance of women’s access to land, property and finance.
**Answer:** Secure access to land, inheritance, savings, credit and productive assets can strengthen bargaining power, investment and resilience. Formal availability is not enough if women lack documentation, collateral, financial literacy, control over loans or influence over household assets. Policy should improve legal awareness, accessible banking, responsible credit, fair inheritance practices, digital finance safeguards and market links. Success should be measured by ownership and control, not merely the number of accounts or loans issued.

### 19. What reforms can make workplaces safer for women?
**Answer:** Employers should establish clear anti-harassment rules, confidential reporting, independent investigation, protection against retaliation, safe transport where needed, adequate sanitation and transparent disciplinary processes. Labour inspectors and regulators require capacity to monitor compliance. Workers need information about rights and access to grievance mechanisms. Measures should cover formal and informal work, including domestic and home-based work where relevant, and must not restrict women’s employment under the guise of protection.

### 20. How should Bangladesh evaluate gender-development programmes?
**Answer:** Begin with a baseline and clearly defined theory of change. Set indicators for access, quality, agency, safety and outcomes; disaggregate data appropriately; protect confidentiality; and involve beneficiaries in evaluation. Distinguish inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes and long-term impacts. Use mixed methods where useful and report unintended consequences. A programme should not be declared successful because it reached many people if learning, safety, income or decision-making did not improve.

### 21. What is gender mainstreaming?
**Answer:** Gender mainstreaming is the systematic assessment of the implications for all genders of planned laws, policies, programmes and budgets. It integrates gender analysis into design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation rather than treating gender as a separate afterthought. Effective mainstreaming needs leadership, technical skills, disaggregated data, assigned responsibility, adequate resources and public reporting. It should complement targeted measures where evidence shows persistent disadvantage.

### 22. Explain the importance of gender statistics.
**Answer:** Gender statistics reveal differences in education, employment, health, safety, time use, assets and decision-making. Without disaggregated and reliable data, policymakers may overlook barriers or confuse participation with equality. Data should have clear definitions, reference periods and representative coverage. Sensitive information, especially violence-related data, requires confidentiality and survivor safety. BBS publications and survey methodology should be cited by name and year; figures from different instruments should not be compared without checking definitions.

### 23. How can men and boys contribute to gender equality?
**Answer:** Men and boys can share care responsibilities, challenge harmful stereotypes, support girls’ education, reject harassment and violence, and promote respectful relationships. Schools, workplaces, community and media programmes can encourage positive role models. Engagement must not shift leadership away from women or treat equality as a favour granted by men. The objective is to transform unequal power relations and establish shared responsibility, dignity and accountability.

### 24. Discuss the role of media in gender equality.
**Answer:** Media can expose violence, scrutinise policy, disseminate rights information and highlight women’s contributions. It can also reinforce stereotypes, invade survivors’ privacy or sensationalise abuse. Responsible reporting requires informed consent, protection of identities, accurate context, non-stigmatising language and avoidance of victim-blaming. Newsrooms should broaden women’s expert representation and track gendered patterns in coverage. Digital platforms also need accessible reporting and transparent responses to abuse.

### 25. “Gender equality is a prerequisite for sustainable development in Bangladesh.” Discuss.
**Answer:** Gender equality expands human capability, improves the use of skills, supports healthier families and strengthens accountable institutions. Bangladesh’s progress in education, health and women’s participation demonstrates the value of investment, while persistent barriers—violence, child marriage, unequal care, unsafe work and unequal access to assets—limit further gains. A sustainable strategy should combine rights-based law, quality public services, decent work, social protection, childcare, women’s leadership, digital inclusion and gender-responsive budgeting. Use official, dated statistics and evaluate outcomes rather than relying on slogans. **Conclusion:** Gender equality is not a side programme; it is a cross-cutting condition for inclusive and resilient development.
