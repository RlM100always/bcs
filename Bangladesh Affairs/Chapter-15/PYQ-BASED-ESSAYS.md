## Essay 1 — “Gender equality is a prerequisite for sustainable development.” Analyse.

**Introduction:** Sustainable development requires economic progress, social justice and the protection of human capabilities. A society cannot use its full human potential when gender-based barriers prevent people from accessing education, health, work, resources and decision-making.

**Analysis:** First, gender equality expands human capital by improving access to education, skills and healthcare. Second, equal access to decent work and productive assets can increase household income and productivity. Third, women’s meaningful participation in public decision-making makes institutions more representative. Fourth, safety and freedom from violence are essential for mobility, education and employment. Fifth, gender-responsive climate adaptation and digital inclusion help ensure that emerging opportunities do not reproduce existing inequalities.

**Bangladesh context:** Girls’ education and women’s participation in several economic sectors demonstrate progress, but child marriage, violence, unequal care responsibilities, unsafe work and barriers to assets remain. These challenges interact with poverty, disability and geography.

**Recommendations:** Implement constitutional guarantees and relevant laws; improve girls’ school completion; expand healthcare, childcare and social protection; enforce safe-workplace standards; increase access to finance and technology; use gender-responsive budgeting and sex-disaggregated data; and evaluate outcomes, not only spending.

**Conclusion:** Gender equality is a cross-cutting development condition. A coordinated approach combining rights, services, economic opportunity and accountability can accelerate inclusive development in Bangladesh.

## Essay 2 — Evaluate the progress and remaining challenges of women’s empowerment in Bangladesh.

**Introduction:** Women’s empowerment means the capacity to access resources, exercise agency, make choices and influence outcomes. It should be assessed through education, health, economic security, safety and decision-making power.

**Progress:** Public policy and community initiatives have widened girls’ educational opportunities and improved access to health and family-planning services. Women contribute to manufacturing, agriculture, services, entrepreneurship, public institutions and civil society. Constitutional guarantees and national policies provide a framework for equality.

**Challenges:** School dropout and child marriage constrain opportunity; unpaid care work reduces time for paid employment; occupational segregation and informal work limit security; violence and harassment restrict mobility; and unequal access to land, finance and digital tools weakens economic autonomy. Formal representation does not always mean meaningful decision-making power.

**Way forward:** Keep girls in school, strengthen skills and technical education, improve affordable childcare, promote equal remuneration, protect workers, expand finance and markets, improve survivor-centred support, and ensure women’s meaningful leadership. Government should publish sex-disaggregated outcomes and evaluate programmes against clear indicators.

**Conclusion:** Progress should not be measured only by enrolment or participation. The decisive test is whether women can live safely, earn fairly, control resources and influence decisions.

## Essay 3 — Child marriage as a development challenge in Bangladesh.

**Introduction:** Child marriage is not only a legal or cultural issue; it affects education, health, agency and intergenerational poverty.

**Causes:** Economic insecurity, social norms, concerns about safety, school discontinuation, crisis and climate shocks, and weak access to services may reinforce the practice. No single cause explains all cases.

**Consequences:** Marriage at a young age may interrupt education, limit skills and employment, increase health risks associated with early pregnancy, and reduce control over life decisions. The effects may extend to children and household wellbeing.

**Response:** Law enforcement must be combined with social protection for vulnerable households, birth registration, safe transport and schools, adolescent-friendly health services, community engagement and confidential reporting. Girls should have access to second-chance education and livelihood pathways.

**Conclusion:** Sustainable prevention requires both protection and opportunity: the law must be enforceable, and girls must have realistic options to continue education and shape their futures.

## Essay 4 — Unpaid care work and women’s economic participation.

**Introduction:** Unpaid care work includes cooking, cleaning, caring for children, older persons and sick family members. Although essential to society and the economy, it is often invisible in conventional measures of production.

**Impact:** Unequal care responsibilities create time poverty, constrain education and paid employment, interrupt careers and reduce opportunities for skills development. This can contribute to income inequality and limit women’s financial independence.

**Policy response:** Expand affordable childcare and care services; encourage men’s participation in household care; improve maternity and parental protections; promote flexible but secure work; collect time-use data; and recognise care work in policy design. Employers and local government can support accessible care facilities.

**Conclusion:** Recognising, reducing and redistributing unpaid care is necessary for a fair labour market. Economic empowerment cannot rely on women adding paid work to an unchanged, unequal care burden.

## Essay 5 — Constitutional and international frameworks for gender equality.

**Introduction:** Rights become meaningful when constitutional principles, domestic laws, institutions and international commitments work together.

**Bangladesh framework:** Articles 27 and 28 of the Constitution provide key equality protections, while Article 29 concerns equality of opportunity in public employment. Relevant domestic laws address child marriage, domestic violence and specified forms of violence against women and children. National policy provides broader direction.

**International framework:** CEDAW establishes obligations to address discrimination against women; the Beijing Platform for Action provides a wide-ranging policy agenda; SDG 5 includes targets on discrimination, violence, unpaid care, leadership, resources and technology.

**Implementation gaps:** Awareness, access to legal aid, survivor protection, institutional coordination, timely justice, data quality and budget accountability can affect implementation. Ratification or enactment alone does not prove outcomes.

**Recommendations:** Publish accessible legal information, improve frontline training, provide safe reporting and referral services, monitor implementation, review policy gaps and report disaggregated results.

**Conclusion:** The test of a rights framework is not its wording alone, but whether it changes people’s safety, choices and access to justice.

## Essay 6 — Gender-responsive budgeting as an accountability tool.

**Introduction:** Gender-responsive budgeting analyses how public revenue and expenditure affect people of different genders. It does not necessarily mean creating a separate budget for women; it means designing and evaluating public spending with gender impacts in view.

**Importance:** It can reveal unequal access to education, healthcare, transport, employment services and social protection. It encourages agencies to identify beneficiaries, outputs and outcomes and to connect allocations with national equality commitments.

**Limitations:** A budget label does not guarantee an effective programme. Weak baseline data, unclear indicators, limited coordination and poor evaluation can make reporting procedural rather than transformative.

**Recommendations:** Set measurable targets; link spending to service delivery and outcomes; publish sex-disaggregated data; include local feedback; audit implementation; and assess both direct and indirect effects.

**Conclusion:** Gender budgeting is useful when it connects resources to measurable improvements in safety, time use, access, income and decision-making—not when it is treated as a reporting exercise.

## Essay 7 — Gender, climate change and disaster management.

**Introduction:** Climate-related hazards interact with social and economic inequalities. Gender is one factor shaping exposure, resources, mobility and recovery, but women are not a homogeneous group and must not be portrayed only as victims.

**Analysis:** Care responsibilities, unequal assets, access to information, safe transport and health services may affect how people prepare for and recover from disasters. Women also contribute local knowledge, livelihood strategies and community organisation.

**Policy:** Include diverse women in local planning; provide safe shelters and sanitation; ensure reproductive and maternal health services; make early warnings accessible; protect against exploitation and violence; and support women’s access to adaptation finance and resilient livelihoods. Collect disaggregated data while protecting privacy.

**Conclusion:** Gender-responsive adaptation is both a justice issue and a practical way to make climate policy more effective.

## Essay 8 — Digital transformation: opportunities and risks for women.

**Introduction:** Digital services can widen access to education, finance, markets and public services, but unequal access and online risks can deepen exclusion.

**Opportunities:** Digital payments, e-commerce, remote learning, online public services and professional networks can help women access income and information, especially where physical mobility is constrained.

**Risks:** Device and data costs, limited digital literacy, household restrictions, privacy risks, online harassment and inaccessible design can exclude women and marginalised groups.

**Recommendations:** Expand affordable connectivity, practical digital training, local-language and accessible content, safe reporting mechanisms, privacy protection and women’s participation in technology design. Digital access should be measured not only by SIM or account ownership but by safe, autonomous and meaningful use.

**Conclusion:** Digital transformation promotes equality only when access is affordable, skills are available and technology is safe and accountable.
